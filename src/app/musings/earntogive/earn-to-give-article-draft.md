# The Coordination Failure of Getting Rich to Do Good

There's a thought experiment that comes up often in conversations about impact: if you're a smart, skilled person who cares about the world's problems, should you go work on them directly — or should you get rich and fund someone else to do it?

The argument for earning to give is seductive. Money is flexible. You can redirect it to whatever's most urgent. And if you're talented enough to earn a lot, maybe the math works out: earn $400k, donate $100k, fund two researchers in a field you couldn't contribute to yourself. Clean, logical, scalable.

But I think this logic has a hidden flaw — one that only becomes visible when you stop looking at individual decisions and start looking at what happens when everyone reasons this way.

## A toy world

Imagine a simplified world. There are 1,000,000 credits of total wealth. 10,000 people. The top 10% — the rich — hold 80% of it. The bottom 90% hold the remaining 20%. Wealth inequality, but nothing unusual by real-world standards.

This world faces 10 critical problems. Climate collapse, pandemics, AI risk — pick your existential threats. Each problem requires two things to solve: 20,000 credits of funding, and 30 skilled workers dedicated to it. Total need across all problems: 200,000 credits and 300 workers.

Now here's the key: this world has 500 skilled people, and 1,000,000 credits. It has *more than enough* of both. Five times the necessary funding. Nearly twice the necessary talent. Every problem is solvable. The resources exist.

So why don't they all get solved?

## The donation bottleneck

The rich donate 5% of their wealth. The poor donate 2%. This is generous by real-world standards — most people give far less. Total donations: 44,000 credits. That's enough to fund about two problems out of ten.

Already, the math is tight. But at least the workers are there, right?

## The defection problem

Here's where it breaks. Each of the 500 skilled people faces a choice: work directly on a problem (modest salary, funded by donations) or go earn in the rich economy (5x the salary, donate their percentage).

If a skilled person chooses to earn, they make 500 credits and donate 5% — that's 25 credits. But the worker they would have replaced costs 50 credits to employ. So it takes *two* skilled earners to fund the salary of *one* skilled worker. Every defection to earning adds 25 credits of funding but removes 50 credits of labor capacity. It's a net loss every time.

But it doesn't *feel* that way to the individual. From their perspective, they see their donation as additive — a contribution on top of whatever everyone else is doing. They don't see that they've removed themselves from a shrinking pool.

## The scenarios

Trace this to its conclusion:

When 300 of the 500 skilled people work directly, there are enough workers for all 10 problems. The bottleneck is funding — donations can only cover about 2. But at least the talent is there, ready to scale if funding increases.

When only 100 work directly, you can staff about 3 problems. The extra funding from 400 skilled earners? It adds about 10,000 credits — moving the needle from 2.2 to 2.7 fundable problems. The marginal money barely matters.

When everyone earns — all 500 skilled people defect to the rich economy — donations increase by 12,500 credits. Total funding: 56,500. But workers available: zero. Problems solved: zero. A mountain of money, and no one left to use it.

## The hardest problems die first

There's a deeper cruelty to this dynamic that the aggregate numbers hide. Not all problems are equally hard. Some need 10 workers, some need 50. Some are politically popular and well-funded; others are obscure and neglected.

As skilled people drain away from direct work, the easy problems get solved first. Clean water, basic sanitation — these attract funding and don't require rare expertise. The hard problems — the ones that threaten civilization, the ones that require decades of focused research by a handful of people who truly understand the science — those are the first to go dark.

The system doesn't fail uniformly. It fails selectively, and it always fails on the problems that matter most.

## So how does this compare to reality?

Here's where it gets uncomfortable. The toy model is generous compared to the real world.

Global wealth is roughly $470 trillion. The top 1% of adults hold about 48% of it. Charitable giving in the US — one of the most philanthropic nations on earth — runs at about 1.8% of income, and that number has barely budged in decades. Globally, the picture is worse. Only about a third of people worldwide report donating money to charity in any given year, and that share has been declining.

In our toy model, the rich donate 5%. In reality, the ultra-wealthy donate closer to 1-2% of their wealth. The world's 3,200 billionaires account for just 8% of individual philanthropy. The total pool of giving is *far* smaller relative to the problems than our model assumes.

And the talent side? Consider the hardest problems facing humanity — climate intervention, pandemic preparedness, AI alignment, antibiotic resistance. Each has perhaps a few hundred to a few thousand active researchers globally, often funded at levels that are rounding errors in the context of national budgets. The gap between research funding and the scale of these problems is not a crack — it's a chasm.

In our model, the world has 5x the funding needed. In reality, the funding allocated to existential risks is maybe 0.01% of what's available. The model assumes 500 skilled people out of 10,000. In the real world, we have millions of PhDs and only a handful working on the problems that could determine whether civilization survives the century.

Our toy world is the optimistic scenario. And even there, the system breaks down.

## The argument

I'm not saying earning to give is wrong. For many people — especially those whose skills don't match the problems that need solving — it's the highest-leverage thing they can do. But the framework breaks when it becomes a universal principle. It breaks when the smartest people all ascend to the top, assuming someone else will stay behind to do the hard work. It breaks when money accumulates in foundations and donor-advised funds while job postings for critical research go unfilled.

The world doesn't need more money. It has $470 trillion. It needs more of the right people willing to deploy their skills on the problems that matter, even when a richer, more legible path is available.

If you're one of those people — if you have the training, the ability, and the inclination to work on something that could matter — consider that your labor might be the scarce resource, not your money. And consider that no one can donate their way into replacing what you would have built.
